Types of failure detected

The four ways a trap wastes steam or hurts the process

Each trap is judged against the saturated steam temperature. Which side of the trap is off, and in which direction, tells the failure apart.

1

Non operational

Inlet▼ Low
OutletNormal

The trap has been manually isolated using its valve. Once isolated it is either choked or bypassed.

ImpactIf choked: waterlogging that affects steam quality and may lead to water hammering. If bypassed: steam losses.
2

Leaking

InletNormal
Outlet▲ High

The trap is leaking steam along with the condensate. The leak rate depends on differential pressure and orifice size.

ImpactLoss of expensive steam. Leak rates are very high in high-pressure header traps and in large-orifice heat exchanger traps.
3

Choking

Inlet▼ Low
OutletNormal

The trap cannot release the condensate. Condensate collects at the inlet and the outlet is not releasing it.

ImpactAffects main steam quality and process temperature, and may lead to hazardous water hammering.
4

Flooding

Inlet▼ Low
OutletNormal

The trap cannot handle the condensate load, although it is still able to release condensate.

ImpactAffects header steam quality and process temperature, and may lead on to choking.

The rule of thumb: a high outlet temperature indicates leaking; a low inlet temperature indicates flooding, a non-operational trap or choking. On heat exchangers, control valve position sets the inlet baseline dynamically.

Steam traps management

Why temperature-only, wireless monitoring

ROI < 1 year

With real-time detection, the time to repair or correct a failed trap drops to a single day, against much longer with manual or third-party audits. With a 10–20% steam trap failure rate, and maintenance made difficult by manpower shortage, dependence on manual or third-party skills, infrequent audits, large numbers of traps and safety concerns, the solution is an ideal fit for the maintenance team.

Temperature-based detection

Inlet and outlet temperatures are monitored to detect leaking, flooding, non-operational and choked traps. A high outlet indicates leaking; a low inlet indicates flooding, a non-operational trap or choking. Saturated steam temperature is used as the baseline. On heat exchangers, control valve position is used to baseline the inlet temperature dynamically.

Lower cost and complexity

Traditional and imported acoustic-plus-temperature solutions bring more complexity and cost than temperature alone to reach a similar outcome, and have largely failed because of surrounding noise, installation requirements and higher cost of ownership.

Standalone and wireless

No cabling. Each node carries two temperature sensors, rated up to 550 °C, mounted on the trap inlet and outlet. The node is battery powered and sends its readings to a gateway over LoRa. The gateway has a SIM card and sends data to the cloud, so the system needs zero cabling for power or communication and zero interfacing with the existing IT/OT network.

DCS / Historian / API integration

Without DCS integration the algorithm uses static baselines, since header pressure and condensate recovery do not vary much. With integration, it runs on real-time process data: header temperature and pressure, control valve position, steam flow, condensate flow and temperature, and heat exchanger process temperature.

Proven and long term

The hardware is designed and field tested to last for many years in corrosive areas, hydrocarbon Zone 1 and all weather, with large-scale implementations in upstream and downstream refineries, pulp and fibre, and pharma. The oldest implementations have been running for more than 4 years.

Installation

Two installation patterns

Header traps need only the node and gateway. Heat exchanger traps can also draw on DCS or historian data to baseline the trap against the live process.

Header trap installation

  1. Inlet and outlet RTDs are mounted on the pipe either side of the trap.
  2. The steam trap monitoring LoRa node mounts on the nearby support pillar.
  3. The trap discharges to the environment or to a condensate recovery line as before.
  4. A 4G LoRa gateway is installed at height in an open location and sends data to the Faclon cloud.

Heat exchanger installation

  1. Inlet and outlet temperature sensors on the trap after the heat exchanger, before the condensate recovery line.
  2. Control valve position, header pressure (e.g. 15 bar), header temperature (e.g. 230 °C) and process temperature come from the DCS or historian.
  3. An industrial PC sends the DCS / historian data to the Faclon cloud over 4G.
  4. Control valve position is used to baseline the trap inlet temperature dynamically.
